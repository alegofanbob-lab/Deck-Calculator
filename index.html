How much does a deck really cost in 2026?

Most homeowners start deck planning with a single question — what's this actually going to cost? — and get answers ranging from $3,000 to $30,000 depending on who's asked. The truth is both numbers can be right. A deck's price is driven by a small number of decisions, and once you know what they are, estimating your own project gets a lot more predictable.

Material is the single biggest lever

Decking material alone can swing your total cost by 2-3x. Pressure-treated pine is the budget baseline — it's affordable, widely available, and holds up well if maintained, but needs re-staining every couple of years. Cedar costs more but resists rot and insects naturally, with a warmer look many homeowners prefer. Composite and PVC sit at the top of the price range, trading a higher upfront cost for essentially zero ongoing maintenance — no staining, no sanding, decades of service life. Which one makes sense depends less on your budget ceiling and more on how you weigh upfront cost against 10-15 years of upkeep.

Size matters less than people expect

It's tempting to assume cost scales directly with square footage, and it mostly does — but not entirely. Larger decks get slightly better per-square-foot pricing on materials (bulk lumber and fastener costs drop a bit at scale), while labor costs scale more linearly. The bigger cost jump usually isn't size — it's height. An elevated deck needs real structural footings, sometimes engineered ones depending on your local code, while a ground-level deck often gets by with simpler post supports. That single decision can add over a thousand dollars before a single board goes down.

DIY vs. hiring a pro

Labor typically makes up 30-45% of a deck's total cost. Building it yourself removes that cost entirely, but it's worth being honest about what "DIY" actually demands: accurate footings are non-negotiable for structural safety, and most local codes require a permit and inspection regardless of who swings the hammer. A realistic middle ground some homeowners choose is hiring a pro for the footings and framing — the part where mistakes are expensive and hard to fix — while handling the decking and railing installation themselves.

The costs people forget to budget for

Three things consistently surprise first-time deck builders: permit fees (often overlooked entirely until the paperwork stage), removal and disposal of an existing structure if you're replacing an old deck, and railing — which is priced per linear foot of perimeter, not included by default in most material cost-per-square-foot figures you'll see quoted online. A deck estimate that only accounts for decking boards and nothing else will consistently land 20-30% under what the project actually costs.

Getting an accurate estimate for your project

National averages are a useful starting point, but your actual number depends on your region, your specific site (a flat, accessible backyard costs less to build on than a sloped or hard-to-reach one), and local permit and labor rates. Use the calculator above to get a realistic range based on your actual dimensions and material choice, then take that number into conversations with 2-3 local contractors as a sanity check on their quotes — not as a substitute for getting real bids.

About these estimates
What updates automatically

Pressure-treated pine and cedar prices track the Federal Reserve's Softwood Lumber Producer Price Index (FRED series WPU0811), refreshed on the 2nd of every month. When lumber costs move, those two material prices move with them — no one has to remember to update this page.

What's reviewed manually

Composite and PVC decking prices, labor rates, footing/foundation costs, and permit allowances aren't tied to any public live-data feed — nothing tracks these as structured data anywhere. These figures are reviewed and adjusted by hand roughly every 3-6 months based on current market pricing.

What's not included

This estimate covers the deck structure itself — framing, decking, railing, footings, and standard permitting. It does not include: removal/disposal of an existing deck, major site excavation or grading, electrical work (outlets, lighting), plumbing hookups, or HOA-specific fees, all of which vary too much by property to estimate meaningfully here.

Why your local quote may differ

These are national ballpark averages. Actual costs shift based on your region (coastal and high cost-of-living areas often run 15-30% higher), site accessibility (a hillside or hard-to-reach backyard raises labor costs), local permit fees, and which specific contractor you hire. Treat this number as a starting point for budgeting and a sanity-check on quotes — not a substitute for getting 2-3 real bids.
